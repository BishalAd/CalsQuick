## Why Tipping Varies Globally

Tipping customs are heavily influenced by local economic structures, labor laws, and cultural values. In some countries, service workers are paid a standard living wage, and tips are seen as a pleasant bonus. In others, tips form the majority of their income.

Before you travel, it is essential to learn the local customs so you can tip fairly without causing offense.

---

## Tipping in North America (US & Canada)

In the United States and Canada, tipping is highly expected in most service industries. 

*   **Restaurants:** 15% to 20% of the pre-tax bill is standard. For exceptional service, 20% to 25% is common.
*   **Bars:** $1 to $2 per drink, or 15% to 20% of the total tab.
*   **Taxis/Rideshares:** 10% to 15% of the fare.
*   **Hotels:** $2 to $5 per night for housekeeping, and $1 to $2 per bag for porters.

## Tipping in Europe

In Europe, tipping is appreciated but generally voluntary, as service charges are often built into prices by law.

*   **Western Europe (UK, France, Germany):** Rounding up the bill or leaving a 5% to 10% tip is common for good restaurant service. If a menu says *Service Compris* (service included), no additional tip is needed.
*   **Southern Europe (Italy, Spain):** Tipping is less common. A service fee (*coperto*) is often added to restaurant bills. Leaving an extra €1 or €2 is a nice gesture for exceptional service.

---

## Tipping in Asia

Tipping customs in Asia vary widely, and in some regions, tipping can be misunderstood or even considered impolite.

*   **Japan:** There is no tipping culture in Japan. Excellent service is considered a standard expectation, and leaving cash tips can be seen as insulting or confusing.
*   **South Korea:** Tipping is not expected and generally not done.
*   **Southeast Asia (Thailand, Vietnam):** Tipping is not traditional, but is becoming more common in tourist-heavy areas. Leaving 5% to 10% in high-end restaurants or rounding up taxi fares is appreciated.
