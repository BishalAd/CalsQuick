## The "8 Glasses a Day" Rule: True or Myth?

You have probably heard that you need to drink eight 8-ounce glasses of water per day (about 2 liters or half a gallon). This is the famous "8×8 rule." But is it scientifically accurate?

The truth is more nuanced. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine recommends:

*   **Men:** About 3.7 liters (125 ounces) of total fluid per day
*   **Women:** About 2.7 liters (91 ounces) of total fluid per day

These numbers include water from all beverages and food (food provides about 20% of daily fluid intake). But your individual needs vary significantly based on multiple factors — which our free **[Water Intake Calculator](/water-intake-calculator)** accounts for.

## How Your Daily Water Needs Are Calculated

The most evidence-based formula for baseline water needs uses body weight:

> **35 ml of water per kilogram of body weight** (or 0.5 ounces per pound)

**Example:** A 70 kg (154 lb) person needs 70 × 35 = **2,450 ml (about 2.5 liters)** of water per day as a baseline.

But this is just the starting point. Your actual requirement adjusts based on:

### 1. Exercise and Physical Activity

*   **Moderate exercise (30–60 min):** Add 400–600 ml (14–20 oz)
*   **Intense exercise (1–2 hours):** Add 600–1,000 ml (20–34 oz)
*   **Endurance sports (3+ hours):** Add 1,000–2,000 ml or more, plus electrolytes

**Practical advice:** Drink 500–600 ml 2 hours before exercise, 150–250 ml every 15–20 minutes during, and 500 ml for every pound of body weight lost after exercise.

### 2. Climate and Temperature

In hot weather above 30°C (86°F), you can lose 1–2 liters of water per hour through sweat. Add 500–1,000 ml extra per day for every 10°C above the comfortable range. People living in tropical climates (like South Asia) may need 4–5 liters daily during summer.

### 3. Special Conditions

*   **Pregnancy:** Add about 300 ml/day. Total recommendation: ~2.3 liters.
*   **Breastfeeding:** Add about 700–1,000 ml/day. Total recommendation: ~3.1 liters, since breast milk is 87% water.
*   **Creatine Supplementation:** During loading phase (20g/day), drink an extra 500–1,000 ml. During maintenance (3–5g/day), add 250–500 ml. Creatine draws water into muscle cells, increasing total body water requirement.

## Water Needs by Age: From Kids to Seniors

| Age Group | Daily Water Recommendation |
|---|---|
| 1–3 years | 1.3 liters (44 oz) |
| 4–8 years | 1.7 liters (57 oz) |
| 9–13 years (boys) | 2.4 liters (81 oz) |
| 9–13 years (girls) | 2.1 liters (71 oz) |
| 14–18 years (boys) | 3.3 liters (112 oz) |
| 14–18 years (girls) | 2.3 liters (78 oz) |
| Adults (men) | 3.7 liters (125 oz) |
| Adults (women) | 2.7 liters (91 oz) |
| Seniors (65+) | 1.7–2.0 liters (57–68 oz) |

**Note for seniors:** Older adults are at higher dehydration risk because thirst sensation weakens with age, kidney function declines, and some medications increase fluid loss.

## Do Coffee, Tea, and Juice Count?

Yes. All beverages contribute to hydration, including coffee and tea. Despite the mild diuretic effect of caffeine, research shows caffeinated drinks still provide net hydration. Sodas, juices, milk, and soups all count too.

However, water remains the healthiest choice — zero calories, no sugar, no artificial additives. Food also provides roughly 20% of daily water intake; fruits and vegetables like watermelon, cucumber, and oranges are 80–95% water.

## Signs You Are Dehydrated (and Overhydrated)

### Dehydration Warning Signs

*   **Mild (1–2% body weight lost):** Thirst, dry mouth, slightly dark urine, headache, reduced concentration
*   **Moderate (3–5%):** Very little urine output, dizziness, fatigue, muscle cramps, dry skin
*   **Severe (>5%):** Rapid heartbeat, sunken eyes, confusion, inability to stand — **seek medical help**

**Quick self-check:** Your urine should be pale yellow. Dark yellow or amber means drink more water. Completely clear urine may indicate overhydration.

### Can You Drink Too Much Water?

Yes — it is called hyponatremia (water intoxication). This occurs when excessive water dilutes sodium levels in your blood. It is rare and mainly affects endurance athletes who drink only water without electrolytes during very long events. For most healthy people, your kidneys can process 800–1,000 ml per hour, so drinking well beyond your calculated need is unnecessary.

## Drink Smarter, Not Just More

Spread your intake throughout the day rather than chugging large amounts at once:

*   **Morning:** 400–500 ml upon waking — rehydrates after 7–8 hours of sleep
*   **Before meals:** 500 ml about 30 minutes before each meal — aids digestion and can reduce appetite
*   **Before/during/after exercise:** Follow the exercise hydration protocol
*   **Before bed:** 200–300 ml — prevents nighttime dehydration without disrupting sleep
