One of the first questions after a positive pregnancy test is: "When is my due date?" The standard method, used by obstetricians worldwide, is Naegele's Rule — add 280 days (40 weeks) to the first day of your last menstrual period. For example, if your LMP was January 1, your estimated due date is October 8. ## How Naegele's Rule Works

The rule is simple: take your LMP date, add 7 days, subtract 3 months, and add 1 year. This assumes a 28-day menstrual cycle with ovulation on day 14. For example, LMP = March 15 → March 15 + 7 days = March 22 → subtract 3 months = December 22 estimated due date.

## What If My Cycle Is Not 28 Days?

If your cycle is longer than 28 days, ovulation occurs later. For a 35-day cycle, ovulation is around day 21 — 7 days later than the standard assumption — so your due date shifts forward by 7 days. Our calculator adjusts automatically based on your cycle length. Irregular cycles make the estimate less reliable; a first-trimester ultrasound provides the most accurate dating (within 5–7 days).

## The Three Trimesters at a Glance

| Trimester | Weeks | Key Milestones |
|---|---|---|
| First | 1–13 | Pregnancy test positive, heartbeat visible (6 weeks), NIPT (10 weeks), NT scan (12 weeks) |
| Second | 14–27 | Quickening/movement felt (16–22 weeks), anatomy scan (20 weeks), viability at 24 weeks |
| Third | 28–40+ | Glucose screening, kick counts begin, full term at 39 weeks, estimated due date at 40 weeks |

## How Accurate Is the Due Date?

Only about 4% of babies arrive exactly on their due date. Approximately 80% are born within two weeks before or after. The due date is an estimate — your healthcare provider will refine it based on ultrasound measurements and your individual health profile.

## Important Medical Disclaimer

This calculator provides an estimate only and is not a substitute for professional medical advice. Always consult your doctor, midwife, or OB-GYN for personalized pregnancy care and accurate dating via ultrasound.
