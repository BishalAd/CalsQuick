If you have been injured at work, understanding your workers' compensation benefits is critical to protecting your financial stability during recovery. Workers' compensation is a mandatory insurance program that covers employees who are hurt or become ill due to their job. Benefits vary by state, injury severity, and your pre-injury earnings — but the core calculation principles are consistent across most jurisdictions.

## What Workers' Compensation Covers

Workers' compensation provides several categories of benefits:

1. **Medical benefits** — All reasonable and necessary medical treatment related to your workplace injury, including doctor visits, surgery, physical therapy, and prescription medication. No co-pays or deductibles for the injured worker.

2. **Temporary Disability (TD) benefits** — Wage replacement while you cannot work during recovery (typically 2/3 of your average weekly wage, up to a state maximum).

3. **Permanent Disability (PD) benefits** — Compensation for lasting impairment after you reach maximum medical improvement.

4. **Supplemental Job Displacement Benefits** — Vouchers for retraining if you cannot return to your pre-injury job.

5. **Death benefits** — Payments to dependents if a work-related injury or illness causes death.

## How Temporary Disability Benefits Are Calculated

Temporary disability is the most common workers' comp benefit. The calculation formula:

**TD Benefit = Average Weekly Wage (AWW) × 2/3**

Most states cap this at a maximum weekly amount (typically tied to the state's average weekly wage).

**Example:**  
An employee earning $900/week (gross) is injured and cannot work:
- TD benefit = $900 × 0.667 = **$600/week**
- If the state maximum is $1,100/week, they receive the full $600

**Example at a higher income:**  
An employee earning $2,400/week:
- Calculated benefit = $2,400 × 0.667 = $1,601
- If the state maximum is $1,100/week, they receive only **$1,100**

## How Average Weekly Wage Is Calculated

Your Average Weekly Wage (AWW) is typically calculated using your earnings from the 52 weeks (1 year) before your injury date.

**Standard method:**
AWW = Total Earnings in Prior 52 Weeks ÷ 52

**Example:** An employee earned $48,100 in the year before their injury:
AWW = $48,100 ÷ 52 = **$925/week**

Some states use a 13-week or 26-week calculation period instead. Part-time workers may have their AWW calculated differently.

**Wages included in AWW:**
- Regular hourly or salary pay
- Overtime earnings (often included, varies by state)
- Bonuses and commissions (some states include, some exclude)
- Tips (some states include)

## Permanent Disability Ratings

If your injury results in lasting impairment, you may receive permanent disability benefits. These are based on a **disability rating** — expressed as a percentage — assigned by a physician after you reach **Maximum Medical Improvement (MMI)**.

**How PD benefits are calculated:**
1. A doctor examines you and assigns an impairment rating (e.g., 15% whole person impairment)
2. State-specific formulas convert this to a dollar amount or number of weeks of compensation
3. The benefit is paid as a lump sum or weekly payment

**Example (California):**  
A 15% permanent disability rating for a worker earning $700/week:
- California assigns a specific number of compensation weeks based on the rating
- At 15% disability: approximately 82 weeks of PD payments
- PD weekly rate: $209/week (2/3 of $313, the max PD wage)
- Total PD benefit: 82 × $209 = **~$17,138**

## What to Do After a Workplace Injury

**1. Report immediately.** Most states have strict deadlines (30–90 days) for reporting injuries to your employer. Delayed reporting can jeopardize your claim.

**2. Seek medical attention.** Your employer's insurer may direct you to a specific doctor (depending on state), especially for initial treatment.

**3. Document everything.** Keep copies of all medical records, doctor notes, employer communications, and benefit payment notices.

**4. Know your return-to-work obligations.** If your employer offers modified duty within your medical restrictions, you may be required to accept it or risk losing TD benefits.

**5. Consult an attorney for serious injuries.** Workers' comp attorneys typically work on contingency (no upfront fees) and can significantly increase benefit outcomes for permanent disabilities, denied claims, or settlement negotiations.
