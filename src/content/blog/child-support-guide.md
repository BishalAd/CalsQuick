Child support ensures that children receive financial support from both parents after separation or divorce. Every state uses guidelines to calculate payments, but the formulas vary significantly. Understanding which model your state uses and what factors courts consider helps you prepare financially. Our [Child Support Calculator](/child-support-calculator) estimates monthly payments for all 50 states using the correct model for each.

## Three Calculation Models Used Across the US

The Income Shares Model (used by 38+ states) combines both parents' incomes and divides child-rearing costs proportionally. The Percentage of Income Model (Texas, Wisconsin, Illinois, Alaska) applies a flat percentage — typically 17-27% for one child — to the paying parent's net income only. The Melson Formula (Delaware, Hawaii, Montana) is the most complex, reserving basic needs income for each parent before calculating support from surplus.

## What Income Counts

Courts consider wages, self-employment income, bonuses, commissions, investment income, rental income, and even workers comp benefits. A new spouse's income is generally not counted. Courts can also impute income — assigning earning capacity to a parent who is voluntarily underemployed to avoid higher payments.

## How Custody Arrangements Affect Support

Sole custody produces the highest support obligation. Shared 50/50 custody reduces but rarely eliminates support — the higher-earning parent may still owe the lower-earning parent to equalize living standards. Even at equal custody, significant income differences trigger support payments in most states.
